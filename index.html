Internal training reference how this tool works

New to this tool? Read this first. The whole point of this tool is one sentence: sit with a builder, point at the one box of opportunity they care about, and say, let me show you a side by side of what you are doing today versus a smart panel, and why it is the better choice. You have run enough of these conversations to know what it feels like when a builder stops talking price and starts talking value, that is the moment this tool is built to produce, every time, not just on your best day. Everything below serves that one sentence. The tool works for both Eaton and SPAN: the equipment is the Smart Panel, and SPAN Energy Intelligence is the intelligence inside it. Switch to Builder View when you present; Internal is for learning.

The method: how to run this

Run this tool the way the best reps sell: teach, tailor, and take control. Teach the builder something they do not already know. Tailor the number to the person in the room. Take control and move the conversation from unit price to total value. The whole point is repeatability: every rep runs the same motion on every builder, so the output is a consistent number and a method the buyer can act on, not a clever one-off that only works when the best rep is in the room. Run it a few times and it stops feeling like a script and starts feeling like the way you naturally talk about this job.

Teach, lead with an insight, not a brochure. The builder believes the traditional panel is the cheap, safe choice. Your job is to reframe that belief. The cheaper-looking box drags expensive equipment, labor, and utility work behind it, adds boxes to the wall, and quietly caps the options they can sell. You are not answering a request for a quote, you are showing them a cost they did not know they were carrying. That reframe is the sale, not the spec sheet.

Tailor, same job, different scoreboard. The same comparison means different things to different people, so point the tool at the number that person signs off on. Purchasing hears direct installed cost. A marketing-minded builder hears differentiation and option revenue. The electrical contractor hears gross-profit dollars. The AHJ hears code and defensible method. Read the room and lead with their number, not yours.

Take control, talk money and total value. Do not wait to be asked about price, and do not retreat to unit price when you are. The cost-benefit view is where you take control: yes, our cost may be higher, and that is fine. A line that works here is, sure, you only care about the bottom line, but do you really, because you should get credit for the extra value you are getting. Hold that constructive tension for a beat, then land on total value per home and per community.

Why this tool helps you sell. You are not selling one panel, you are handing the builder a clear business decision. Name the box, build today's baseline, show two scenarios, and land on total value. Run the same motion every time and the number holds up, the story stays consistent, and the builder's team can say yes. The value is a defensible number and a repeatable method, not a hero pitch. Everything below is that motion, step by step, and by the time you finish reading it you should already be able to picture the exact builder you're going to run it with next.

Say this every time. This is a conversation, not a quote, not a bill of materials, not an estimate. It shows the job holistically so the builder sees there is more to it than gray box versus white box. Beat that drum with every builder, and with every rep you teach to use this tool.

Step 1: The opportunity (the four boxes)

Builders do not buy a panel, they solve a problem. There are four problems, and we call them the four boxes: solar and storage, avoiding a larger than 200A service, building codes, and option enablement. Your first move is to name the box the builder is actually in. Once you pick it, the tool only asks the questions that matter for that box and recommends the right comparison. How it impacts the outcome: the box you pick decides which scenarios we compare and which value we take credit for at the end.

Step 2: What are you doing today

We are always judged against today, or against what the builder thinks today has to be, so we start there. Do not build the smart panel solution first and then try to reverse into their current setup. Ask a few simple questions (what are you specifying today, is the panel inside or outside, do you have a subpanel, how many homes, what size) and let the tool pre-build the traditional architecture. Then you get to say, here is what I believe you are doing today, and let them confirm or correct it. That is where our expertise shows. Most of the time this is the second conversation, when we already know their plans and their electrical contractor. How it impacts the outcome: this becomes the baseline that every dollar is measured against.

Select two scenarios

Keep it to two, with an optional third, because a builder can only really hold three comparisons in their head. In production, almost everyone who goes up goes to 400A, so the core comparison is 400A traditional versus a 200A smart panel. 320A barely exists, and the utility often will not even give it, and 600A is a custom, last-resort scenario. The most important optional third is the 200A traditional load center, because that is the true apples to apples for the option-enablement story: same service, so the only difference is the intelligence. How it impacts the outcome: the two you pick are the whole conversation, so pick the ones that match this builder, and nail the production case first.

When the job goes past 200A (400A or 600A Smart Panel Solution)

Some jobs need more capacity than one smart panel covers. For those, the tool now shows a 400A or 600A Smart Panel Solution, two smart panels working together, lined up against the matching traditional service the same way the 200A comparison works. Use it any time production or custom pushes past 200A and a single panel is not enough, the same avoid-a-bigger-service story you already know how to tell now covers the bigger jobs too. Two things here are still placeholders until we get real field numbers: the extra labor hours for the second panel, and whether the two panels are wired as one shared service or each need their own disconnect. Both are editable and flagged in the tool. If you run one of these live, note what you actually saw in the field and send it back, that is exactly the data that finalizes this for everyone after you. How it impacts the outcome: the story you already tell at 200A now scales to the jobs that used to be harder to make simple.

The comparison: material, labor, utility, installed cost

Every column is built the same way and the sections line up: material, then labor, then utility and site, then installed cost. That alignment is on purpose, so the builder's eye can go straight down one column and across to the other and see exactly what changes. The smart panel column will have a lot of white space where the traditional column has a stack of equipment. White space is a feature, not a gap; it makes us look simpler, because we are.

Material: the only real hardware difference is the number of panels and boxes. A 400A job is a meter main plus two 200A sub-panels, maybe a CT cabinet, maybe a disconnect, and downstream gear. A smart panel job is one box at 200A, or two smart panels on a 400A or 600A Smart Panel Solution job, still fewer boxes and less to explain than the traditional stack. Breakers are the same on every option, so they never decide the comparison.

Labor: this is the part people get wrong. Labor here is not roping the whole house. The rough and trim wiring of outlets, lights, and switches is the same no matter what, so it does not count. What we count is the work on the garage wall: terminating in the panel, feeder wires, and hanging boxes. Two boxes take longer to hang, and to split circuits between, than one smart panel. We give the smart panel an honest extra hour for commissioning and show it right next to the traditional hours so it is a fair, side by side line.

Utility and site: this is the land-dev cost, the utility upgrade, trenching, and laterals. It only shows up on the paths that raise the service, which is exactly why avoiding a larger service is worth money.

How it impacts the outcome: this is where the builder sees, in their own numbers, that the cheaper-looking traditional panel drags a lot of expensive equipment and labor behind it.

CT cabinet and specialty equipment

A production builder does not know what a CT metering cabinet is and does not want to, so specialty equipment only appears when the service size actually calls for it. A CT cabinet is a 400A-and-above item and depends on the utility, and it is guaranteed at 600A. The tool notes that on the 400A column and lets you zero it out if the utility does not require it. How it impacts the outcome: we keep the production view clean and only surface detail that is real.

Home / Project Type

This is context only and does not change labor, pricing, or markup. Production is a standardized, repeatable build. Spec home is more variation in configuration, loads, and options. Custom is a project-specific architecture with greater flexibility. It describes the home, not the labor. Labor hours are entered manually per scenario, so the rep stays in control of the number. How it impacts the outcome: the type frames the conversation without inflating any side.

Labor and material, and the adjustment lever

Pick the state and the tool pulls a regional installer labor benchmark behind the scenes and applies a standing builder markup (30 percent by default, editable in the internal view). Material is estimated from square footage the same way, with margin included. The builder never sees a raw hourly rate or a material factor. If a builder says the averages run high or low, use the Cost adjustment percent to nudge the whole estimate up or down without exposing the underlying pricing. How it impacts the outcome: the numbers stay regionally honest and defensible while the specific pricing stays internal.

Breaker and SPD schedule (collapsed, at the bottom)

Breakers are the same in every architecture, so they are never the deciding delta. We keep the schedule collapsed and near the bottom. It is there to show we did our due diligence, and it is really for the electrical contractor conversation, where MSRP versus volume pricing builds trust on their supply costs. A production builder does not need to see it. How it impacts the outcome: it stays out of the builder's way but is there when the contractor wants it.

Two buckets: direct cost, then additional value

Keep it simple. For the whole project we are really looking at three things: hardware, labor, and incentives. The first bucket is direct installed cost, which is hardware plus labor plus utility, and it is what purchasing cares about. Rebates work against that bucket: a rebate comes right off the top of the Smart Panel installed cost, shown as gross cost, less rebate, net, so purchasing sees the real number they are paying. The second bucket is additional value: option enablement and Smart Home Value Opportunity. That value is not cost, so it stays separate and never touches the installed number. Finance-minded builders call these above the line and below the line, and you can use that language, but you do not have to; direct cost and additional value works just as well. One caution: rebates can expire or get fully subscribed, so keep the gross installed cost in view and never let the case depend on the rebate alone. How it impacts the outcome: the rebate lowers the Smart Panel installed cost head to head, and the enabled value is clearly labeled as extra.

Option enablement

This is the biggest play in the 200A base case: managed capacity lets the builder offer options they could not offer before without a bigger service, from a second EV charger to a steam shower to a hot tub or an ADU circuit. Add options from the list or a custom one, set a value, or leave it at $0. Values are subjective, so use an industry average. If a builder challenges a number, change it or take it to zero right there. That is the whole game: credibility, not hoodwinking. These values and any rebates apply to the Smart Panel scenario, since that is what enables them; the difference is that option and Smart Home Value Opportunity value stays in this separate bucket, while a rebate comes off the installed cost directly. How it impacts the outcome: this is where we take credit for value the traditional panel simply cannot enable.

Smart Home Value Opportunity, and why it defaults to a conservative number

This one confuses people, so here is the plain version. Think about how a car and a home electrical system are opposites in one specific way: a car loses value the second it drives off the lot, but capacity and control on a home's electrical system stay with the property, and it can grow. A smart panel gives a home more usable capacity, the ability to manage the loads on it, and room for what a homeowner adds later, an EV charger, solar, storage, without a truck roll and a service upgrade. That argument stands on its own, you do not need a percentage to make it, and you should lead with it every time.

The percentage is a different thing. We went and looked, there is no study of a smart panel specifically, so never present this number as researched or guaranteed for this product. What does exist, and it is real, is a body of large, controlled studies on comparable electrical and energy infrastructure: energy-rated new homes selling for 2.7 percent more across roughly 450,000 matched properties (Freddie Mac), green-certified homes at a 3.46 percent mean premium in an appraiser-led paired sales study (IMT and DC's Department of Energy and Environment), and solar-equipped homes at a 0.87 percent premium in a Fannie Mae and Lawrence Berkeley Lab study of over half a million transactions. Against that real range, roughly 1 to 5 percent, this tool now starts at 1 percent, the conservative end. Say it exactly that way if you're asked: comparable infrastructure has shown measured premiums in this range, and we start low.

It stays fully editable. Move it up only when the builder gives you a number themselves, in their own words, because they see the differentiation or marketability case. If they push back, take it to zero and lean on the capacity and optionality argument, which needs no number to be true. How it impacts the outcome: you always have the infrastructure argument in your pocket, the percentage is a bonus a builder hands you, never something we hand them.

Cost-benefit analysis (one conclusion)

This is the single conclusion picture, and there is only one of them, never a separate bar for cost and another for benefit, because that confuses people. Cost is one color, benefit is another. The point: our cost may be higher, and that is fine, because the builder sees more benefit for it. This is the moment you stop selling a cheaper panel and start selling a better business decision. This is also where you hold a little constructive tension: sure, you only care about the bottom line, but do you really, because you should get credit for the extra value you are getting. How it impacts the outcome: this is the view that changes the decision criteria.

Per home and per community

Always show both. Per home is what they feel. Per community is per home times the number of homes, and it is impact, not profit, so we never call it profit. Take real credit here: across the community you are reducing cost, adding home value, and unlocking option revenue. The common mistake is not taking enough credit on the community number. How it impacts the outcome: the community view is the number that carries the business case for the builder.

The margin conversation (EC and Internal view)

This is the contractor's economics, and it is the one that flips a contractor from resisting a premium panel to pushing it. Contractors price on markup percentage out of habit. Here is the exact way this was run with a real contractor: how much do you charge for a regular panel, and what is your markup. They said 22 to 25 percent. On a $100 panel that is $25, tops. Now do the same on a smart panel. Even if you drop your markup way down to 8 percent, you are still making five to six times more dollars on that one panel. The tool shows it: gross profit dollars on each side and the cost to the customer on both. Two rules from Eaton and from leadership. First, the top control is Production versus Custom, and you have to be careful exposing production pricing during a custom conversation, so use it deliberately. Second, we do not add a separate line for the cost to carry a unit, because a contractor's overhead is already baked into their margin, and stacking it again would create a false expectation. How it impacts the outcome: this is how the contractor becomes an advocate for the smart panel.

Terminology, because both Eaton and SPAN use this

The equipment is the Smart Panel and the intelligence is SPAN Energy Intelligence, so say smart panel with SPAN Energy Intelligence. When it is just the equipment, say smart panel, or smart panel Main 40, or MLO 48. A downstream intelligent panel is a smart subpanel. Never say gray box, say traditional load center, and never say SPAN panel or SPAN hardware, because it could just as easily be an Eaton panel. Eaton is capitalized, smart panel is lowercase, SPAN is all caps, and Energy Intelligence is capitalized.

Assumptions, legal, and handling a challenge

Every default is a labeled assumption and everything is editable, on purpose. This is a sales conversation and value model, not a quote and not an engineering document, and we are not the Engineer of Record. Outcomes are subject to AHJ approval and utility acceptance. When you get challenged, treat it as a gift: change the number, and the conclusion becomes theirs.
