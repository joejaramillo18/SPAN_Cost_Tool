Internal training reference how this tool works

New to this tool? Read this first. The whole point of this tool is one sentence: sit with a builder, point at the one box of opportunity they care about, and say, let me show you a side by side of what you are doing today versus a smart panel, and why it is the better choice. Everything below serves that one sentence. The tool works for both Eaton and SPAN: the equipment is the Smart Panel, and SPAN Energy Intelligence is the intelligence inside it. The method: how Karen wants you to run this

This tool is a Challenger sale on a page, so run it like one. A Challenger does three things: teach, tailor, and take control. Teach the builder something they do not already know. Tailor the number to the person in the room. Take control and move the conversation from unit price to total value. Karen builds it this way for one reason a VP of sales cares about above all else: repeatability. Every rep runs the same motion on every builder, and the output is a number and a method a purchasing leader or a VP can sign off on, not a clever one-off that only works when the best rep is in the room.

Teach, lead with an insight, not a brochure. The builder believes the traditional panel is the cheap, safe choice. Your job is to reframe that belief. The cheaper-looking box drags expensive equipment, labor, and utility work behind it, adds boxes to the wall, and quietly caps the options they can sell. You are not answering a request for a quote, you are showing them a cost they did not know they were carrying. That reframe is the sale, not the spec sheet.

Tailor, same job, different scoreboard. The same comparison means different things to different people, so point the tool at the number that person signs off on. Purchasing hears direct installed cost. A marketing-minded builder hears differentiation and option revenue. The electrical contractor hears gross-profit dollars. The AHJ hears code and defensible method. Read the room and lead with their number, not yours.

Take control, talk money and total value. Do not wait to be asked about price, and do not retreat to unit price when you are. The cost-benefit view is where you take control: yes, our cost may be higher, and that is fine. The line Karen uses is, sure, you only care about the bottom line, but do you really, because you should get credit for the extra value you are getting. Hold that constructive tension for a beat, then land on total value per home and per community.

Why a VP runs it this way. Karen is not selling one panel, she is building a repeatable motion the whole team can run and the forecast can trust. Name the box, build today's baseline, show two scenarios, land on total value. When every rep does that the same way, the number is defensible, the pipeline is real, and the builder's leadership can say yes. A VP signs off on a method and a number, not on a hero pitch. Everything below is that motion, step by step.

Step 1: The opportunity (the four boxes)

Builders do not buy a panel, they solve a problem. There are four problems, and we call them the four boxes: solar and storage, avoiding a larger than 200A service, building codes, and option enablement. Your first move is to name the box the builder is actually in. Once you pick it, the tool only asks the questions that matter for that box and recommends the right comparison. How it impacts the outcome: the box you pick decides which scenarios we compare and which value we take credit for at the end.

Step 2: What are you doing today

We are always judged against today, or against what the builder thinks today has to be, so we start there. Do not build the smart panel solution first and then try to reverse into their current setup. Ask a few simple questions (what are you specifying today, is the panel inside or outside, do you have a subpanel, how many homes, what size) and let the tool pre-build the traditional architecture. Then you get to say, here is what I believe you are doing today, and let them confirm or correct it. That is where our expertise shows. Most of the time this is the second conversation, when we already know their plans and their electrical contractor. How it impacts the outcome: this becomes the baseline that every dollar is measured against.

Select two scenarios

Keep it to two, with an optional third, because a builder can only really hold three comparisons in their head. In production, almost everyone who goes up goes to 400A, so the core comparison is 400A traditional versus a 200A smart panel. 320A barely exists, and the utility often will not even give it, and 600A is a custom, last-resort scenario. The most important optional third is the 200A traditional load center, because that is the true apples to apples for the option-enablement story: same service, so the only difference is the intelligence. How it impacts the outcome: the two you pick are the whole conversation, so pick the ones that match this builder, and nail the production case first.

The comparison: material, labor, utility, installed cost

Every column is built the same way and the sections line up: material, then labor, then utility and site, then installed cost. That alignment is on purpose, so the builder's eye can go straight down one column and across to the other and see exactly what changes. The smart panel column will have a lot of white space where the traditional column has a stack of equipment. White space is a feature, not a gap; it makes us look simpler, because we are.

Material: the only real hardware difference is the number of panels and boxes. A 400A job is a meter main plus two 200A sub-panels, maybe a CT cabinet, maybe a disconnect, and downstream gear. A smart panel is one box. Breakers are the same on every option, so they never decide the comparison.

Labor: this is the part people get wrong. Labor here is not roping the whole house. The rough and trim wiring of outlets, lights, and switches is the same no matter what, so it does not count. What we count is the work on the garage wall: terminating in the panel, feeder wires, and hanging boxes. Two boxes take longer to hang, and to split circuits between, than one smart panel. We give the smart panel an honest extra hour for commissioning and show it right next to the traditional hours so it is a fair, side by side line.

Utility and site: this is the land-dev cost, the utility upgrade, trenching, and laterals. It only shows up on the paths that raise the service, which is exactly why avoiding a larger service is worth money.

How it impacts the outcome: this is where the builder sees, in their own numbers, that the cheaper-looking traditional panel drags a lot of expensive equipment and labor behind it.

CT cabinet and specialty equipment

A production builder does not know what a CT metering cabinet is and does not want to, so specialty equipment only appears when the service size actually calls for it. A CT cabinet is a 400A-and-above item and depends on the utility, and it is guaranteed at 600A. The tool notes that on the 400A column and lets you zero it out if the utility does not require it. How it impacts the outcome: we keep the production view clean and only surface complexity that is real.

Project complexity

This scales labor for the harder jobs, and it is defined, not a mystery percentage. Standard or production is your typical job, one or two panels at 200A. Moderate is a bigger service with extra distribution and a few large loads. Complex or custom is 320A and up with multiple panels, unusual architecture, or custom construction. Think service size, number of panels, circuit count, overhead versus underground, and how many HVAC systems. Important: complexity only adds labor to the multi-panel comparison scenario, like a 400A with two sub-panels, not to a standalone smart panel, because a single panel on the wall is not the complex part. If the smart panel later carries a downstream sub-panel, it can pick up complexity too. How it impacts the outcome: it keeps the labor honest without inflating the smart-panel side.

Labor rate

This is the average national electrical labor rate, changeable if preferred. Remember that production builders pay a lot less than national retail, because their electricians work piece rate, so if you know the real number, use it. How it impacts the outcome: labor is a real part of the delta, so an honest rate keeps the whole comparison credible.

Breaker and SPD schedule (collapsed, at the bottom)

Breakers are the same in every architecture, so they are never the deciding delta. We keep the schedule collapsed and near the bottom. It is there to show we did our due diligence, and it is really for the electrical contractor conversation, where MSRP versus volume pricing builds trust on their supply costs. A production builder does not need to see it. How it impacts the outcome: it stays out of the builder's way but is there when the contractor wants it.

Two buckets: direct cost, then additional value

Keep it simple. For the whole project we are really looking at three things: hardware, labor, and incentives. The first bucket is direct installed cost, which is hardware plus labor plus utility, and it is what purchasing cares about. The second bucket is additional value and incentives: rebates, option enablement, and home value or marketability. We keep the two separate on purpose. Rebates can expire or get fully subscribed, so the case can never depend on them, and value is not cost, so it never touches the installed number. Karen calls these above the line and below the line, and you can use that language with a finance-minded builder, but you do not have to; direct cost and additional value works just as well. How it impacts the outcome: the direct-cost case stands on its own, and the extra value is clearly labeled as extra.

Option enablement

This is the biggest play in the 200A base case: managed capacity lets the builder offer options they could not offer before without a bigger service, from a second EV charger to a steam shower to a hot tub or an ADU circuit. Add options from the list or a custom one, set a value, or leave it at $0. Values are subjective, so use an industry average. If a builder challenges a number, change it or take it to zero right there. That is the whole game: credibility, not hoodwinking. Rebates and these values apply to the Smart Panel scenario, since that is what enables them. How it impacts the outcome: this is where we take credit for value the traditional panel simply cannot enable.

Home value and marketability (and why it is optional)

This one confuses people, so here is the plain version. It is not guaranteed home appreciation, and we never claim it is. It is an optional credit for the simple fact that a smart-panel home is easier to market and sell: it is future-ready, it carries the technology and app the homeowner wants, and it lets the builder differentiate and offer options competitors cannot. Some builders, especially marketing-minded ones, will pay attention to that; a pure purchasing conversation will not. So it is off by default and set to $0. Turn it on and enter a number only when the builder buys the differentiation argument, and if they push back, take it to zero. Treat it as the softest number in the tool, and lead with the hard costs first.

Cost-benefit analysis (one conclusion)

This is the single conclusion picture, and there is only one of them, never a separate bar for cost and another for benefit, because that confuses people. Cost is one color, benefit is another. The point: our cost may be higher, and that is fine, because the builder sees more benefit for it. This is the moment you stop selling a cheaper panel and start selling a better business decision. It is also the Challenger moment: sure, you only care about the bottom line, but do you really? Let me challenge that, because you should get credit for the extra value you are getting. How it impacts the outcome: this is the view that changes the decision criteria.

Per home and per community

Always show both. Per home is what they feel. Per community is per home times the number of homes, and it is impact, not profit, so we never call it profit. Take real credit here: across the community you are reducing cost, adding home value, and unlocking option revenue. The common mistake is not taking enough credit on the community number. How it impacts the outcome: the community view is what a purchasing leader or a VP signs off on.

The margin conversation (EC and Internal view)

This is the contractor's economics, and it is the one that flips a contractor from resisting a premium panel to pushing it. Contractors price on markup percentage out of habit. Here is the exact way this was run with a real contractor: how much do you charge for a regular panel, and what is your markup. They said 22 to 25 percent. On a $100 panel that is $25, tops. Now do the same on a smart panel. Even if you drop your markup way down to 8 percent, you are still making five to six times more dollars on that one panel. The tool shows it: gross profit dollars on each side and the cost to the customer on both. Two rules from Eaton and from leadership. First, the top control is Production versus Custom, and you have to be careful exposing production pricing during a custom conversation, so use it deliberately. Second, we do not add a separate line for the cost to carry a unit, because a contractor's overhead is already baked into their margin, and stacking it again would create a false expectation. How it impacts the outcome: this is how the contractor becomes an advocate for the smart panel.

Terminology, because both Eaton and SPAN use this

The equipment is the Smart Panel and the intelligence is SPAN Energy Intelligence, so say smart panel with SPAN Energy Intelligence. When it is just the equipment, say smart panel, or smart panel Main 40, or MLO 48. A downstream intelligent panel is a smart subpanel. Never say gray box, say traditional load center, and never say SPAN panel or SPAN hardware, because it could just as easily be an Eaton panel. Eaton is capitalized, smart panel is lowercase, SPAN is all caps, and Energy Intelligence is capitalized.

Assumptions, legal, and handling a challenge

This is a sales conversation and value model, not a quote and not an engineering document, and we are not the Engineer of Record. Outcomes are subject to AHJ approval and utility acceptance. When you get challenged, treat it as a gift: change the number, and the conclusion becomes theirs.

Internal training reference: the B2B logic how this side works

The B2B side answers a different question than the Builder side. The customer here is the installer or dealer, and the question is: what is my upside from converting a Traditional Load Center job into a Smart Panel job. Everything is built to show margin per job, then per year, plus the rebate.

Run it as a Challenger sale, and think like a VP about the year, not the job

Same method as the Builder side: teach, tailor, take control. Teach the contractor the thing they do not see, that pricing on markup percentage is quietly costing them money, because a smaller percentage on a smart panel still puts several times more gross-profit dollars in their pocket than a fat percentage on a traditional load center. Tailor to the shop in front of you with the Production versus Custom control. Take control by leading with the dark impact box and the annual value plus rebate, not headline revenue. The reason Karen frames it around the year is that a VP does not close a job, she builds a book of business: repeatable margin, a rebate that stacks on volume, and a customer kept for the future work. On MPU plus Service Upgrade, do not hide the negative per-job delta, reframe it out loud. SPAN with PowerUp may allow you to land below a full service upgrade, so the customer saves, you win the price-sensitive job you would have lost today, and you keep them for every future load. That is the close-rate and future-revenue win, and it is the number that actually moves total annual value.

The core model

For each project type we build both sides of the job the same way. The Smart Panel side starts from the installer's cost, sells at MSRP, and marks up labor and hardware at the entered margins. The Traditional Load Center side is built the same way from the traditional materials and labor. The difference between the two prices is the added revenue per job, and the difference between the two margins is the added margin per job. Lead with the dark impact box, not the inputs.

The three project types

Main Panel Upgrade is a one-for-one swap, and the Smart Panel adds real margin plus every future load the homeowner adds later. MPU plus Service Upgrade is the honest one: a Smart Panel with PowerUp can land below a full service upgrade, so the added revenue and margin can come out negative. That is not a loss, it is a close-rate and future-revenue win, and you say that out loud. Sub-Panel compares a traditional sub against a Smart Subpanel, which is monitored, controllable capacity instead of dead capacity.

From per job to per year

Projects per month, the conversion rate, and the mix across project types turn a per-job number into an annual number. On top of that sits the SPAN Installer Program rebate: under 60 units a year is nothing, 60 to 119 is $75 per unit, and 120 or more is $150 per unit. The Total Annual Value is added revenue plus the rebate. Lead with margin and the rebate, not headline revenue.

Production vs Custom

The business-type control sets the margin assumptions. Production uses volume margins, Custom uses higher margins, and both drive the same margin logic. Use it to match the shop you are actually talking to.
